An AI Launch Is Not an Adoption Strategy

How I helped a major technology client build employee awareness and understanding of a new AI initiative.

AI had arrived. Employee understanding still needed an introduction.

For a major technology client, I delivered an internal communication campaign supporting a new AI adoption initiative. The job was to help employees understand what was changing, why it mattered and where the new capabilities could fit into their work.

The reported starting point was 0% awareness and understanding of the specific initiative—not AI in general. Within one quarter, both measures exceeded 70%. Subsequent surveys put both above 80%.

That was the clearest result: a measurable increase in understanding, not just another announcement in the inbox.

The challenge: “New technology” is not a reason to care

An AI launch can explain what a tool does without answering the question employees are quietly asking: “What does this have to do with my work?”

My communication task was to make that connection.

The initiative needed an explanation that went beyond capabilities and connected with everyday responsibilities, existing workflows and client pain points. Employees needed to understand the approach before they could make an informed decision about using it.

The campaign focused on four questions:

  • What is this initiative?
  • Why does it matter to my role?
  • How could it fit into the tools and workflows I already use?
  • Where could it help me address a client’s problem?

The aim was not to make AI sound impressive. It was to make its relevance easier to understand.

My role: translate the initiative, not the jargon

I delivered the internal communication campaign supporting the launch.

My contribution centred on explaining the new approach and connecting it with practical work. That meant keeping the communication focused on employee relevance rather than treating the launch itself as the story.

The objectives were straightforward:

  1. Build awareness of the initiative.
  2. Improve understanding of its purpose and potential application.
  3. Support the move towards practical use.
  4. Measure changes through internal pulse and employee surveys.

I owned the communication contribution—not the technology, technical integration or every factor influencing adoption. A credible case study should know where its own job ends.

The approach: make it relevant, then measure understanding

Start with work, not wonder

The campaign connected the AI initiative with employees’ existing responsibilities and client needs.

The focus was on how the capabilities could work alongside existing enterprise products and workflows. This gave the communication a practical centre: not “look what AI can do”, but “consider where this could help”.

Give awareness and understanding separate seats

Knowing that an initiative exists is not the same as understanding it.

The measurement therefore tracked both. Awareness showed whether the initiative had registered. Understanding showed whether employees were making sense of the new approach.

Neither was treated as a synonym for adoption. Recognising the gym is not the same as going.

Use surveys, not applause

Internal pulse surveys and employee surveys provided the reported measurement points: the baseline, the first-quarter result and a subsequent assessment.

That gave the campaign a clearer account of progress than “the launch went well”—a phrase that can cover anything from genuine understanding to excellent catering.

The results: a substantial knowledge gain

Both measures increased by more than 70 percentage points within one quarter, then subsequently exceeded 80%.

A small but important distinction: that is a percentage-point increase, not a 70% relative increase. Starting from zero makes the latter calculation undefined. The maths deserves clear communication too.

What about engagement and adoption?

I also reported a 90% employee engagement score and early use of the AI capability alongside existing enterprise products.

Those observations belong in the wider account, but they need their own supporting detail:

  • Engagement: what exactly did the 90% score measure?
  • Initial adoption: how many eligible employees used the capability, and within what period?
  • Workflow integration: which tasks changed, and how?
  • Client impact: which pain points were addressed, with what result?

Until those details are documented, the awareness and understanding figures remain the principal quantified outcomes.

No metric should be asked to do three jobs because it looks good in a headline.

What I can credibly claim

I delivered an internal communication campaign during a period in which reported employee awareness and understanding rose from 0% to above 70% within one quarter, subsequently exceeding 80%.

The results are consistent with the campaign’s objectives. They do not isolate communication from other influences, such as training, technology access, manager support or peer learning. Nor do they establish sustained adoption, productivity gains or financial ROI.

That does not make the result insignificant. It makes the claim specific.

The lesson: make the change understandable

This case reflects how I approach AI enablement: connect the initiative with people’s work, explain its relevance and measure whether understanding has improved.

A launch puts a capability in front of people. Communication helps them make sense of it. Adoption is the next question—not a result to assume because the announcement landed.

Evidence note: The client is anonymised. Figures are practitioner-reported from internal surveys.
