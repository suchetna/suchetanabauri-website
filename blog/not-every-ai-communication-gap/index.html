Not every AI communication gap is a communication problem

Leaders have explained the AI strategy. Employees are still asking what they are allowed to do on Monday. Before commissioning another campaign, find out whether you have a missing message, an unusable explanation or a decision nobody has made.

Suchetana Bauri17 November 202612 min read

The AI town hall has gone well. The chief executive has said “opportunity” several times. The presentation contains an upward arrow. Someone has demonstrated a task that previously took three hours and now takes twelve seconds, excluding preparation, checking and the other things that happen outside demonstrations.

Then an employee asks: “Can I use it to summarise a client meeting?”

The answer should be straightforward. It is not.

The technology lead says the enterprise tool is approved. Information security says approval depends on the data. The manager says the team has been encouraged to experiment. Someone offers to circulate the policy.

The policy says to exercise appropriate judgement.

Judgement, meanwhile, is waiting for instructions.

This is an illustrative scene, not a reported incident. Its purpose is to expose a distinction that a successful town hall can conceal: an organisation can communicate its ambition without resolving what people should actually do.

Sometimes employees have missed the message. Sometimes they have read it but cannot apply it. Sometimes they understand the problem rather better than the people presenting the solution: there is no agreed answer.

These are different failures. Sending another email treats them as one.

The view from the presentation is excellent

There is evidence that leaders and employees experience AI change differently. There is also a temptation to make that evidence say rather more than it does.

In a November 2025 Harvard Business Review article, Deborah Lovich, Stephan Meier and Chenault Taylor reported survey findings. While 76% of executives said their employees felt enthusiastic about AI adoption, only 31% of individual contributors expressed enthusiasm themselves. That is a striking gap between leaders’ perceptions and employees’ reported feelings. It is not a comprehension test. An employee can understand the strategy perfectly and dislike it with considerable precision.

Gallup’s June 2025 research gets closer to the practical problem. Among US employees, 44% said their organisation had begun integrating AI, but only 22% said it had communicated a clear plan or strategy. Thirty per cent reported general guidelines or formal policies for workplace AI use. These are employee reports, not an inspection of each organisation’s documents or a direct test of understanding. Still, they give little reason to assume that introducing AI automatically brings usable direction with it.

Gallup also identified an “unclear use case or value proposition” as the most commonly reported adoption challenge. Only 16% of employees who reported using AI strongly agreed that their organisation’s AI tools were useful for their work. Before blaming a workforce for insufficient enthusiasm, it seems reasonable to establish whether the proposed benefit has become recognisable.

The question for communicators is therefore not simply: “Did we explain the strategy?”

It is: “Can people connect it to a decision in their own work?”

As I have argued in AI needs better stories, not louder promises, access to a powerful tool and an understanding of where it belongs are separate achievements.

Three gaps, three different jobs

A change-comprehension diagnostic starts with a modest proposition: before fixing a misunderstanding, establish what kind of problem it is.

The three categories below are a proposed working method, not a validated scientific instrument. Their value lies in separating questions that require different owners and responses.

1. The message never reached the moment of need

“I didn’t know there was an approved tool.”

Perhaps the employee missed the announcement. Perhaps contractors could not access the page. Perhaps the guidance was published under a department name nobody outside that department would think to search.

The document exists. This is excellent news for the document.

It tells us less about the employee’s ability to find it.

Investigate access, timing, targeting and retrieval. Ask people to locate the current guidance rather than merely asking whether they remember receiving it.

The remedy might be a better-targeted message. It might also be a better-labelled page, fewer competing versions or a direct link from the place where the task happens.

This is the connection between communication and design explored in The interface owes you an explanation: before writing another instruction, examine the system that apparently needs explaining.

2. The explanation arrived, but cannot be used

“I’ve read the policy. I still don’t know whether these meeting notes count as confidential.”

Here, delivery may have worked. The employee can repeat the instruction but cannot translate it into an action.

“Protect sensitive information” expresses a principle. It does not necessarily resolve whether a particular document may enter a particular tool for a particular purpose.

Test application with a realistic scenario. What information would the employee check? Which tool would they use? When would they stop and ask?

If an authorised answer exists, the response may involve clearer examples, a decision aid or practice with feedback. Repeating the original principle in a larger font is unlikely to supply the missing distinction.

But check the underlying guidance first. An explanation cannot make a genuinely ambiguous policy unambiguous by force of personality.

3. The organisation has not decided

“Who is accountable if the AI-generated summary leaves out something important?”

Now the employee may have reached the edge of leadership’s own thinking.

Ask the relevant owners independently. Inspect the approved guidance. If legal, operations and technology give incompatible answers, do not record the employee as confused.

Record the decision as unresolved.

The next step is to identify who can settle it, what instruction applies meanwhile and when people will hear more.

A communication team can make a decision intelligible. It cannot make the decision exist.

Why “communicate more” can make things worse

More communication is not inherently harmful. Communicating uncertainty honestly is often exactly what is needed.

The problem begins when repetition gives an unsettled position the appearance of authority.

Imagine that “experiment responsibly” becomes a campaign slogan before anyone agrees which work is suitable for experimentation. One manager interprets it as permission to use any available tool. Another permits only the enterprise tool. A third suspends experimentation altogether.

The organisation now has three local policies and one centrally approved phrase.

Producing more material around that phrase may deepen the contradiction. Employees can reasonably wonder why something described so confidently becomes so negotiable when applied.

If guidance changes, explain what changed and why. A revised rule prompted by a known risk is different from an earlier announcement that concealed an unresolved decision. People deserve to know which has happened.

The sharper response to a difficult employee question may therefore be less copy, not more: a decision meeting, an accountable owner and an interim instruction.

Sometimes the sentence refuses to become clear because the organisation has not agreed what it means.

Stop confusing understanding with obedience

A person can understand a change and oppose it.

They might think the proposed review process is inadequate. They might see that time saved by one team becomes checking work for another. They might question whether a new productivity expectation leaves enough time to verify the output.

Those are propositions to examine, not symptoms to correct.

A useful diagnostic separates three questions:

  • What do you understand the expectation to be?
  • What would you do in this situation?
  • What concerns do you have about that expectation?

The first tests interpretation. The second tests application. The third invites judgement.

Combining them into “Are you clear and confident about our AI journey?” produces a pleasantly manageable answer to several different questions. It is less useful for discovering which one needs attention.

Confusion should not be labelled resistance. Equally, agreement should not be counted as comprehension. An enthusiastic employee may still misunderstand the data rules. A sceptical employee may apply them impeccably.

The diagnostic is not an applause meter.

Managers should not have to invent the missing policy

Managers belong in the assessment as a distinct group, not merely as the route through which leaders’ messages travel.

Ask what they believe the change requires, which questions they can answer and which they have been answering provisionally. Compare those answers with leadership’s authorised position.

A manager who cannot explain an unsettled rule is not necessarily a weak communicator. They may be accurately representing the available clarity.

For each important question, distinguish:

  • What managers should explain consistently.
  • What they may adapt to local work.
  • What they must escalate rather than decide themselves.

This places responsibility more fairly. It also gives communication teams something more useful to produce than a slide deck managers must somehow convert into operational certainty.

The broader principle is set out in The communication problem at the heart of digital transformation: an announcement needs an ongoing system of explanation, local interpretation, feedback and adjustment. The diagnostic checks where that system needs attention.

Six areas worth testing

The aim is not to establish whether employees have memorised leadership’s vocabulary. It is to discover whether they have enough practical understanding to act, question or pause appropriately.

Purpose: what problem are we solving?

Ask: “What is this AI change supposed to improve?”

“Making us AI-first” is a description of technological enthusiasm, not a work problem.

Useful answers name something recognisable: reducing time spent assembling routine reports, improving access to approved information or helping teams prepare a first draft.

If leaders disagree about the intended outcome, employee explanations cannot be expected to converge neatly.

Role impact: what changes in my work?

Ask: “Which part of your work changes, and what remains your responsibility?”

Separate generating an output from checking it, approving it and acting on it. A tool may produce a summary without taking responsibility for its omissions.

“AI will support you” leaves the division of labour politely unspecified. People need to understand the actual handover.

Expectations: what must I do differently?

Ask: “Is using this tool optional, encouraged or required for this task?”

Then test what follows. Is learning time available? Has a deadline changed? Are employees expected to disclose AI use? Does choosing not to use it require an explanation?

Distinguish an invitation to experiment from a performance expectation. They are different arrangements, even when both appear beneath a heading about empowerment.

Boundaries: what is allowed here?

Ask about a specific task, data type and tool.

“Can I paste client meeting notes into the approved workspace?” is more revealing than “Do you understand responsible AI?”

The correct answer depends on the organisation’s rules and circumstances. The diagnostic should not invent a universal permission. It should establish whether the authorised position exists and whether people can apply it.

Support: where does the question go?

Ask: “What would you do if you were unsure?”

An employee might know where to report a login failure but not where to ask whether a proposed use is acceptable. Those are different support needs.

Test the route, not just awareness of it. Can the person find the channel? Is ownership clear? Does the answer resolve the question or simply direct them elsewhere?

Uncertainty: what is still undecided?

Ask leaders: “Which material questions can you not answer yet?”

Then ask employees what they believe remains open.

This is the area that prevents the exercise from becoming another audit of employee recall. Leadership has to participate in the examination.

For each unresolved issue, record an owner, the current interim position and a next-update date. A final decision date may not yet be credible. A commitment to provide an update can still be explicit.

Do not rank these six areas by presumed frequency before collecting evidence. Prioritise according to consequence: a small number of dangerous boundary misunderstandings may demand attention before a widespread inability to recite the programme’s purpose.

Compare for meaning, not wording

Suppose one employee says: “I can use the approved workspace, but not my personal account.”

Another says: “Work information must stay in the company-authorised environment.”

Depending on the actual policy and task, these may express equivalent practical understanding. Neither person needs to reproduce the original sentence.

Now suppose someone says: “Anything is allowed if I remove the client’s name.”

That answer needs investigation. Does the approved guidance support it? What other information remains? Where did the interpretation come from?

Use a simple coding framework:

CodeWhat it indicates
AlignedThe answer captures the essential meaning and supports an appropriate action.
PartialIt captures some essentials but misses a relevant condition or next step.
MissingThe participant cannot identify the relevant guidance or expectation.
ContradictoryIt describes a materially different instruction, responsibility or action.

Add a separate cause tag where the evidence supports one: access problem, unusable explanation, conflicting guidance or unresolved decision. Leave room for “insufficient evidence”.

Crucially, establish the authorised position before judging alignment. Where leadership itself disagrees, that disagreement is a finding—not a reason to choose the most senior answer as truth.

Nor should the four codes become one impressive-looking comprehension percentage. A misunderstanding about a campaign theme and a misunderstanding about sensitive data do not become equally important because both fit into a spreadsheet.

Run a diagnostic people can answer honestly

Start with one defined rollout, workflow or business unit. “Our entire AI transformation” is an ambitious scope for discovering what somebody means by “approved”.

For a small qualitative pilot, a practical starting design could include four to six leaders, six to ten managers and 20 to 30 employees. These are proposed planning ranges, not statistically established requirements. Selection matters: include different tasks, locations, access needs and levels of AI use, rather than relying entirely on enthusiastic volunteers.

Combine a short open-text questionnaire with targeted interviews and realistic scenarios. Review current guidance alongside the answers. Interview leaders independently before bringing them together; otherwise, the meeting may manufacture the agreement you intended to test.

A two- to three-week exercise may be feasible once access and scope are agreed. Larger or higher-risk programmes will need a different design.

Explain the safeguards before asking for candour. Individual responses should not become performance evidence. Report themes without exposing participants through unnecessarily specific job descriptions. Avoid collecting sensitive work material, explain confidentiality limits and do not promise anonymity that a small team makes impossible.

The purpose is to test the organisation’s explanations and decisions, not to catch employees failing an examination they did not know they were taking.

The report should end arguments, not become another document

A useful gap report separates communication work from policy decisions, process fixes and learning needs.

It should show what was tested, who participated, the limitations, the differences between groups and the actions required.

An illustrative entry might look like this:

Report itemExample
FindingParticipants give conflicting answers about processing client meeting notes.
Required actionConfirm the permitted workflow before extending the pilot.
Interim message“This pilot does not currently authorise using client meeting notes.”
Manager talking point“Please do not create local exceptions. Raise proposed uses through the pilot support route.”
Open decisionUnder what conditions, if any, may these notes enter the approved tool?
Owner and dateNamed policy and process owners, with an agreed calendar date for the next update.

This is fictional guidance, not a recommended policy. In a real engagement, the relevant owners would approve every instruction before publication.

Follow the changes with a targeted re-test: can participants now apply the revised guidance to comparable situations? Are managers still receiving questions nobody owns?

A four- to six-week check might suit a pilot, depending on when the changes actually reach people. Report what improved within the sample. Do not convert that into a claim that communication caused higher productivity.

What this cannot tell you

A small diagnostic cannot certify organisation-wide comprehension, prove compliance or establish why every employee uses—or avoids—AI.

It does not replace security assessment, legal advice, skills evaluation or examination of the workflow itself. Someone may understand the instructions and still lack time, access or a workable tool.

That limitation is useful. It stops communication becoming the department to which every inconvenient implementation problem is forwarded.

The exercise offers something narrower and actionable: evidence about where sampled groups interpret the change differently, where guidance fails in practice and where a decision is missing.

The research discussed earlier supports examining these questions. It does not validate this particular method or promise a business outcome.

Before the next email, ask two questions

You do not need a consultancy engagement to begin.

Ask three leaders, three managers and three employees, independently:

  1. What are people expected to do differently because of this AI change?
  2. What should they do when they are unsure whether a proposed use is allowed?

Treat this as a conversation starter, not a representative assessment.

Compare the practical meaning of the answers. Where they differ, investigate. Did someone miss the information? Does the explanation fail when applied? Or has the organisation not decided?

Then give the problem to the person who can solve it.

The employee who asks an awkward question may not need a clearer message. They may have found the decision your launch plan stepped around.

Before you send another message, find out which gap you have.
